Florida Child Support: How It’s Calculated and When It Can Change

By Jennifer M. Erlinger, Esq. · Erlinger Family Law · Serving Duval, Clay, St. Johns and Nassau Counties

“My hours got cut.” “My ex got a big raise.” “The payments just stopped.” Three different homes, one question: what does Florida law say about child support?

Child support is one of the most searched family law topics for a reason: it touches the monthly budget of almost every parent. Whether you pay it, receive it, or are about to be ordered into it, the rumors travel fast, and many of them are wrong.

In more than 22 years of helping Northeast Florida families, I’ve seen how much calmer parents feel once they understand the actual rules. Florida child support is built on a formula in Florida Statutes §61.30, and changes to an existing order are controlled by §61.14. Here is the plain-English version for families in Duval, Clay, Nassau and St. Johns County.

1. How Florida calculates child support

Under §61.30, the court starts with each parent’s monthly net income, adds the two together, and looks up the basic support need for the number of children in a statutory schedule. Each parent is then assigned a percentage share based on their share of the combined income. Work-related child care costs and the child’s health insurance and uncovered medical costs are added to the calculation.

The result is called the guideline amount, and it is presumed to be the correct amount of support. A judge may vary it only slightly (generally up to 5 percent) unless the court makes written findings that the guideline amount would be unjust or inappropriate.

“The formula is the starting line. The facts you can document are what move the number.”

2. What counts as income?

The statute’s definition of gross income is broad. It includes wages, bonuses, self-employment and business income, disability and retirement payments, interest, dividends, rental income and more. Allowed deductions include income taxes, FICA or self-employment tax, certain mandatory payments, health insurance premiums for the parent, and support actually paid for other children.

If a parent is voluntarily unemployed or underemployed, the court can impute income, meaning it can calculate support using what that parent could reasonably earn. And if a parent doesn’t participate or doesn’t provide adequate financial information, the statute allows the court to presume an income level. Complete, honest financial records protect you.

3. Why overnights matter

Under §61.30, a parent has a “substantial amount of time” when they exercise time-sharing for at least 20 percent of the overnights in a year, roughly 73 overnights. When both parents have that much time, the court uses a shared-time formula that takes each parent’s overnights into account, rather than the standard formula.

This is one reason time-sharing and child support are connected, even though they are decided separately. Support cannot be traded for time with your child, and time with your child cannot be withheld because of unpaid support.

4. Can child support be changed?

Yes, but not automatically and not by agreement between the parents alone. To change an existing order, a parent generally has to file a supplemental petition for modification and show a substantial change in circumstances. Under §61.30(1)(b), when the change is based on the guidelines, the difference between the current order and the new guideline amount must be at least 15 percent or $50, whichever is greater.

Examples that may support a modification include a significant, involuntary change in income, a change in the number of overnights, or a change in child care or health insurance costs. The statute also provides that a parent’s failure to regularly exercise the court-ordered time-sharing schedule can be a substantial change, with the adjustment dated back to when it began.

Child support generally continues until the child turns 18, or until 19 if the child is still in high school and performing in good faith with a reasonable expectation of graduating before turning 19.

5. Why you should never “just stop paying”

Under §61.14, a court can generally modify support only for installments that come due after the date you file for modification. Payments missed before you file usually remain owed. Unpaid installments become delinquent, can add up quickly, and can be enforced through the court, including contempt proceedings.

If your situation has changed, the safest move is to file, not to fall behind. The sooner a petition is filed, the earlier a modification can take effect.

“If your income changed yesterday, the clock on a modification starts the day you file, not the day your paycheck dropped.”

6. A simple checklist

  • Gather income records: recent pay stubs, tax returns, and records of any bonuses or side income.
  • Track overnights. Keep a calendar of the actual schedule.
  • Document costs: child care, health insurance and out-of-pocket medical expenses.
  • Keep paying what is ordered while you seek a change.
  • Keep communication in writing and avoid informal side agreements about money.

Frequently asked questions

How is child support calculated in Florida?

Under §61.30, the court combines both parents’s net incomes, applies a schedule for the number of children, divides the amount by each parent’s share of income, and adds child care and health insurance costs.

Can I lower my child support if I lost my job?

Possibly, if the change is substantial and the guideline difference meets the 15 percent or $50 threshold, but only a court can change the order, and generally only going forward from the date you file.

Does having more overnights lower child support?

It can. When each parent has at least 20 percent of the overnights, Florida uses a shared-time formula that accounts for both parents’ time.

What if the other parent stopped paying?

Unpaid support can be enforced through the court. Keep records of what was ordered and what was paid, and talk to an attorney about your options.
