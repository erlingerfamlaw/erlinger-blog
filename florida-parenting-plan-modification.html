Florida Parenting Plans: Can You Change Yours?

By Jennifer M. Erlinger, Esq. · Erlinger Family Law · Serving Duval, Clay, St. Johns and Nassau Counties

“Your parenting plan was written for the family you had then.”

A schedule that worked when your child was three may not work at ten. A new job. A move. A new school. A work shift that changed everything. Suddenly, a plan that once made sense feels like a weekly argument.

If you’re dealing with a difficult divorce, fighting for time with your children, or stuck with a plan that no longer works, you don’t have to keep going in circles. This guide is for you. Whether you’re a mom or a dad, whether you’re going through a divorce or already have a final judgment, here is what every Florida parent should know about parenting plans and modifications.

What many Florida parents don’t know

  • ⚖️ Your parenting plan is a court order, not a suggestion. Read more
  • 📝 A one-time swap can be agreed in writing, but a permanent change generally needs a court-approved modification. Read more
  • 📂 Informal “handshake” changes don’t change the order. The original plan still counts. Read more

1. What is a parenting plan, exactly?

In Florida, a parenting plan is the document that governs how divorced or separated parents share responsibility for their children. It includes a time-sharing schedule (regular weekly time, holidays, school breaks and summer) and how major decisions are made. The rules come from Florida Statutes §61.13.

Once the court approves it, it’s not a suggestion. It is a legal order, and both parents are expected to follow it.

2. Can you change it?

Yes, but how depends on what you’re changing.

  • A one-time swap (for example, trading a weekend): parents can usually agree to this in writing, as long as it’s safe and both are comfortable. Put it in writing so there’s no confusion later.
  • A permanent change to the schedule or decision-making: this generally requires asking the court to modify the parenting plan, even if both parents agree.

The “handshake agreement” trap

Many parents quietly change the schedule for months or years because it works for everyone. But informal “handshake” changes don’t change the court order. If one parent later decides to follow the original plan, the original plan is what counts. Without a court-approved modification, the order is still the order.

“If you’ve been living by a handshake agreement, the court order may still say something very different.”

3. When is a modification possible?

Generally, a parent asking the court to modify a time-sharing schedule or parenting plan needs to show a substantial change in circumstances and that the change is in the child’s best interests. Common examples include:

  • A parent’s work schedule or job has changed
  • A parent has moved, or plans to move
  • The child’s needs have changed with age, school or health
  • The current schedule isn’t being followed, or has stopped working
  • Safety concerns have arisen

Florida changed this area of law in 2023 (effective July 1, 2023). Among other changes, the law removed the requirement that the change be unanticipated when modifying a parenting plan or time-sharing schedule, and it provides that a parent’s move to within 50 miles of the child’s primary residence may be considered a substantial change in circumstances. Whether your situation qualifies depends on your facts, which is exactly why a conversation matters.

What this means for you: if you’ve been told in the past that your situation “isn’t enough” to change a time-sharing schedule, or you haven’t looked at your plan since before July 2023, it may be worth a fresh look.

4. What about Florida’s equal time-sharing presumption?

Under the 2023 amendment to §61.13, there is now a rebuttable presumption that equal time-sharing is in a child’s best interests. A parent who opposes equal time-sharing must prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that it’s not in the child’s best interests.

That doesn’t mean every plan is automatically 50/50, and it doesn’t erase your current order. But if your plan was created before the change, it may be worth asking how the new law applies to your family.

5. What if the other parent isn’t following the plan?

If a parent keeps ignoring the schedule, a court can be asked to step in through a motion for enforcement and/or contempt. Depending on the facts, remedies can include make-up time or an order that the violating parent pay part of the other parent’s attorney’s fees. Keep records: dates, times and messages, kept calm and factual.

6. A simple checklist before you act

  • Re-read your current parenting plan and highlight what isn’t working.
  • Write down what changed and when: job, address, school, health, schedule.
  • Gather records of missed or changed time-sharing.
  • Keep communication civil and in writing.
  • Talk to a family law attorney before filing or agreeing to anything permanent.

Frequently asked questions

Can we change our parenting plan without going to court?

Parents can often agree to a one-time change in writing. A permanent change generally requires a court-approved modification.

Do both parents have to agree to a modification?

No. One parent can ask the court to modify the plan, and the court decides based on the law and the child’s best interests.

Does Florida automatically give 50/50 time-sharing now?

No. Florida law now presumes equal time-sharing is in a child’s best interests, but the presumption can be rebutted, and existing orders stay in effect until a court changes them.

How long does a modification take?

It varies widely. Agreed modifications can be resolved more quickly than contested ones. An attorney can give you a realistic estimate for your situation.
