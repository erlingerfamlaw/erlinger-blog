Moving With Your Child in Florida? The 50-Mile Rule Every Parent Should Know

By Jennifer M. Erlinger, Esq. · Erlinger Family Law · Serving Duval, Clay, St. Johns and Nassau Counties

“A new job in another city. A fresh start closer to family. A move that feels like the only way forward. But if your child is moving with you, Florida law has rules, and breaking them can cost you.”

Maybe you were offered a job in Atlanta. Maybe you want to move back near your parents after the divorce. Maybe your co-parent just told you they’re moving and the kids are going with them, and you’re hearing about it for the first time.

Whichever side of this you’re on, moving a child is not like moving a couch. If you share time-sharing with the other parent, Florida has a specific law that controls when and how a parent may relocate with a child: Florida Statutes §61.13001, Parental Relocation with a Child.

In more than 22 years of helping Northeast Florida families, I’ve seen how quickly a “simple move” becomes a legal emergency when the rules aren’t followed. Here’s what every parent in Duval, Clay, Nassau and St. Johns County should know.

What many Florida parents don’t know

  • 📍 A “relocation” means moving your principal residence at least 50 miles away for 60 or more consecutive days. Read more
  • 📝 You generally need either a signed written agreement or a court petition before you move with your child. Read more
  • ⏱️ If you’re served with a petition to relocate, you have 20 days to respond. Read more
  • ⚠️ Moving without following the law can lead to contempt, a court order to return the child, and attorney’s fees. Read more

1. What counts as a “relocation” in Florida?

Under §61.13001, a relocation is a change in a parent’s principal residence that is at least 50 miles from where they lived when the last time-sharing order or the initial filing was made, and that lasts at least 60 consecutive days. Temporary absences for things like vacation, education or a child’s health care do not count.

So a family vacation to Colorado is not a relocation. A new home in another state or even another part of Florida more than 50 miles away may be.

“Moving 50 miles can change a child’s school, friends, routine and every other weekend. The law takes that seriously, and so should you.”

2. Two legal ways to relocate with your child

Option 1: A written agreement. If all parents and others entitled to time-sharing agree, they can sign a written agreement that reflects their consent to the move, sets a new time-sharing schedule, and describes transportation arrangements for the child. If there’s already a court case or order, the other parent can ask for a hearing within 10 days after the agreement is filed. If no one asks, the move is presumed to be in the child’s best interest.

Option 2: A petition to relocate. If you don’t have an agreement, the parent who wants to move must file a sworn Petition to Relocate and have the other parent formally served. The petition must include things like the new address, the reason for the move, and a proposed new time-sharing schedule and transportation plan. If the reason is a job offer, the written offer must be attached. A petition that leaves out required items can be found legally insufficient.

3. If you’ve been served with a petition to relocate

The petition will include notice language warning that you must respond within 20 days after service. If you don’t file a timely, verified objection, the law presumes the move is in the child’s best interest and the court may allow it. If you do object, the parent who wants to move may not relocate until the court grants permission.

Your objection must be verified and must state the specific facts behind your objection, including your involvement in your child’s life. Twenty days goes quickly. If you’re served, don’t wait: call an attorney the same week.

4. How does a judge decide?

When a move materially affects the current time-sharing schedule, Florida law does not start with a presumption for or against the move. The parent who wants to relocate has the initial burden to prove the move is in the child’s best interest, and then the burden shifts to the other parent to show it is not. The judge considers factors such as:

  • The child’s relationship with each parent, siblings and extended family
  • The child’s age, needs and development
  • Whether the child’s relationship with the other parent can be preserved through a new schedule
  • The child’s preference, depending on age and maturity
  • Whether the move will improve quality of life for the parent and child
  • Whether each parent is acting in good faith and meeting their obligations

5. The risk of moving first and asking later

If a parent moves a child without following the statute, the other parent can ask the court to act. The consequences can include contempt of court, an order to return the child, the violation being counted against the parent in the relocation case or in modifying the parenting plan, and an order to pay the other parent’s attorney’s fees.

6. A simple checklist before any move

  • Read your parenting plan and time-sharing order. Note what it says about moves and travel.
  • Measure the distance. Is the new home 50 miles or more from the current one?
  • Talk first, in writing. A signed, filed agreement can protect everyone.
  • Gather the facts: the new job offer, the new school, housing, and a realistic long-distance time-sharing plan.
  • Don’t move the child first. Talk to an attorney before you sign a lease or enroll in a school.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need permission to move within Florida with my child?

If the move is at least 50 miles from your principal residence and lasts 60 or more consecutive days, the relocation statute generally applies, even within Florida.

Can I move if the other parent agrees?

Yes, but the agreement should be in writing, signed by everyone entitled to time-sharing, and include a new time-sharing schedule and transportation plan.

How long do I have to respond to a petition to relocate?

The petition must state that you have 20 days after service to file a verified objection.

What if the other parent moved my child without telling me?

You may be able to ask the court for relief, including a temporary order restraining the move or ordering the child’s return. Time matters, so talk to an attorney right away.
